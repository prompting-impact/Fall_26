Course Vision

The Premise

AI is no longer peripheral to design culture. It is reshaping how architects and designers research, visualize, communicate, evaluate, and justify decisions.

Its greatest challenge, however, is not technical alone. It is ethical, interpretive, and disciplinary. Prompting Impact positions AI as a live professional condition already transforming practice, pedagogy, and public discourse.

The Response

The course advances a model of AI education grounded in professional relevance without collapsing into software training.

Students use generative tools as meaningful collaborators in ideation, research, communication, and design development — while preserving the primacy of human insight and direction. Rather than celebrating speed for its own sake, the seminar trains students to identify what matters, frame what is at stake, and communicate design intelligence with precision and credibility.

Professional Premise

→Interpret complex and often fragmented information without losing the human scale of design decisions.
→Translate research, evidence, and design intent into narratives that can influence clients, institutions, and publics.
→Represent people, communities, environments, and futures responsibly within AI-mediated workflows.
→Use acceleration strategically while preserving authorship, critique, and professional accountability.
→Work in multidisciplinary teams that mirror contemporary practice by integrating design, computation, media, ethics, and systems thinking.

Modular Course Structure

All students complete two long core modules and two short intensives selected from a rotating set. The structure establishes a common conceptual foundation while allowing differentiated pathways.

Core 01 · 7 Weeks
01

AI-Mediated Ethics

Bias · Representation · Authorship · Disclosure · Accountability

Frames ethics as an infrastructural condition of AI-assisted design rather than an afterthought. Students examine bias, visibility, dataset politics, authorship, disclosure, and representational harm, then test how ethical judgment can become an active design capacity.

Possible Outputs Ethics audits · Case-study analyses · Disclosure protocols · Synthetic media critiques
Core 02 · 7 Weeks
02

Narrative Intelligence and the Client Mindset

Strategic Storytelling · Stakeholder Framing · Design Argument

Positions storytelling as a professional design instrument. Students learn to translate spatial research, technical insight, and conceptual ambition into persuasive narratives legible to clients, institutions, and public audiences. Emphasis on framing, sequence, evidence, tone, and strategic use of media.

Possible Outputs Stakeholder maps · Client briefs · Pitch decks · Narrative prototypes
A
Short · 2 Weeks
AI as Design Accelerator

Testing where AI genuinely expands design capacity and where it produces false speed. Prototype hybrid workflows across precedent analysis, ideation, iteration, comparative testing, and communication.

Outputs: workflow maps · iteration logs · precedent-to-prototype studies
B
Short · 2 Weeks
Reading Big Data at the Human Scale

Interpreting large or messy datasets without flattening human experience. The focus is spatial translation: surface patterns, relationships, and tensions that can inform design while retaining cultural nuance and local specificity.

Outputs: workflow maps · iterative concept studies · reflective process audits
C
Short · 2 Weeks
Glitch, Noise, and the Uncanny

This module treats error, ambiguity, and machine failure as productive design tools. As generative AI improves, identifying and creating salient glitches amid AI slop becomes a moral imperative. Glitches expose the limits of machine reasoning while opening space for critique, speculation, and disciplinary invention. They are fascinating, often beautiful artifacts of imperfect systems.

Outputs: curated error archives · uncanny spatial studies · speculative proposals
D
Short · 2 Weeks
Living Systems and Ecological Intelligence

AI supporting environmentally responsive design by interpreting climate, material, landscape, and building-performance conditions as interconnected systems. Moves beyond generalized sustainability toward precise environmental consequence.

Outputs: ecological systems maps · scenario studies · material response diagrams · short design proposals

Major Deliverables & Grading

Major deliverables reflect the course structure: two long module projects, short module deliverables, a public-facing showcase, and sustained participation in workshops, critiques, and peer exchange.

01 · Long Module 1 Project15%

Students complete a foundational project that establishes the course’s core framework: human-centered AI, design intelligence, prompting, datasets, authorship, ethics, and workflow development. This project builds the conceptual and methodological base for the rest of the semester and helps students understand AI as both a design tool and a critical design context.

02 · Short Module Deliverables40%

Students are introduced to four short modules across the semester taught by visiting faculty. Each short module is worth 10% and should include a refined output, process documentation, AI workflow evidence, and a short critical reflection explaining the module’s design relevance, ethical implications, and connection to the student’s larger inquiry.

03 · Long Module 2 Project15%

Students develop a second long-form project that integrates the lessons, methods, and tools introduced across the semester. This project supports the transition from modular experiments into a coherent design/research argument. Students should demonstrate how their selected short modules inform their design position, representational strategy, and human-centered contribution.

04 · Public Showcase Presentation20%

Students present a final synthesis of their semester-long work through a curated exhibition during the Public Showcase on December 4. Presentations should clearly communicate the project’s central question, design process, AI workflow, ethical stakes, human-centered implications, and design contribution.

05 · Attendance, Participation, and Critique10%

Students are expected to attend class, arrive prepared, participate actively in workshops and discussions, contribute meaningfully to critiques, and support the development of peer work. Because this course is structured around shared experimentation, feedback, and public exchange, participation is considered an essential part of the learning process.

Total100%

The grading structure balances design inquiry, modular investigation depth, public communication, and sustained course engagement.

Featured Work · Core Module A
Atlas of AI
Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence · Yale University Press, 2021
Kate Crawford ↗
Crawford maps AI not as software but as geography — tracing the mines, server farms, warehouses, and bodies that make machine learning possible. The atlas form is deliberate: AI has spatial, material, and political coordinates that its interfaces hide. Crawford exposes extraction at every layer — lithium and cobalt from the earth, labor from crowdworkers and fulfillment centers, data from users who did not consent to its use as training material. For the ethics module, this is the grounding text: before asking what AI does in design practice, students must account for what it costs and who bears that cost. The book also provides the visual and rhetorical model for how to make systems legible — a direct input into the data literacy and narrative intelligence modules.
Research Framework · Core Module A
Algorithmic Bias, Accountability, and Independent AI Research
Founder and Executive Director · Distributed Artificial Intelligence Research Institute
Distributed AI Research Institute ↗
Gebru’s research examines algorithmic bias and the institutional accountability required when AI systems shape representation, access, and public life. As founder and executive director of the Distributed Artificial Intelligence Research Institute (DAIR), she advances independent, community-rooted AI research outside Big Tech. For the course, her work connects dataset critique and representational harm to questions of labor, power, institutional responsibility, and the development of more accountable research practices.
Featured Work · Core Module A
Race After Technology
Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code · Polity Press, 2019
Ruha Benjamin ↗
Benjamin coins the concept of the "New Jim Code" — the way that technically neutral-seeming systems encode racial hierarchy through the assumptions, data, and design choices embedded in them. Her argument is not that technology is inherently racist but that it inherits the social conditions of its production, and that the neutrality claim actively prevents correction. For designers working with AI tools trained on existing datasets, Benjamin's framework is essential: the images, spatial precedents, and typologies most represented in training data reflect historical patterns of inclusion and exclusion that will be reproduced unless actively interrogated. The ethics module uses her work to ask what it means to exercise representational responsibility at the point of prompt, output, and publication.
Featured Work · Core Module A
Algorithms of Oppression
How Search Engines Reinforce Racism · NYU Press, 2018
Safiya Umoja Noble ↗
Noble's study of Google's search algorithm demonstrates that information retrieval systems are not neutral mirrors of existing knowledge but active producers of hierarchy — surfacing some images, narratives, and bodies as default and normal while marginalizing others. Her analysis of what the search engine returned for queries involving race and gender was a landmark intervention in how technologists, critics, and designers talk about bias: not as an accident or edge case, but as a structural feature of systems optimized for engagement over accuracy or equity. For the image politics and synthetic realism sessions in the ethics module, Noble provides the analytical frame: when AI image tools produce generic, whitened, or stereotyped outputs, that is not a bug — it is a documented tendency of optimization under unexamined training conditions.
Featured Work · Design Culture
Are We Human? Notes on an Archaeology of Design
Lars Müller Publishers, 2016 — co-authored with Mark Wigley
Beatriz Colomina ↗
Colomina and Wigley ask whether design is a species characteristic — something that defines and has always defined what it means to be human — and trace this argument through the Paleolithic, the modernist project, and the emergence of interaction design and smart objects. The book refuses the standard origin story of design as a twentieth-century professional practice and instead situates every object, tool, and environment as a form of self-design: humans design themselves by designing their world. For the course, this reframes the stakes of AI in design practice — if design is how humans constitute themselves, then the delegation of design judgment to AI systems is not a workflow question but an anthropological one. Colomina's earlier work on architecture and mass media also informs the synthetic realism and image politics sessions.
Featured Work · Design Culture
Are We Human? Notes on an Archaeology of Design
Lars Müller Publishers, 2016 — co-authored with Beatriz Colomina
Mark Wigley ↗
Wigley's contribution to this collaboration extends his longstanding interrogation of what architecture claims to be and what it actually does — a critique always aimed at the gap between professional self-description and material consequence. His writing on networked architecture, the electromagnetic environment, and the way design disciplines construct authority through image and language is directly relevant to the narrative intelligence module: what does it mean to communicate architecture, and to whom, and in whose interest? For the course's emphasis on design as an argument — made to clients, institutions, and publics — Wigley provides the theoretical scaffolding for taking communication not as a supplement to design but as design itself.
Featured Work · AI + Architecture
The Second Digital Turn + Beyond Digital
Design Beyond Intelligence, 2017 · Design and Automation at the End of Modernity, 2023 · MIT Press
Mario Carpo ↗
Carpo’s recent work helps frame a new way of thinking about design after the digital turn. In The Second Digital Turn, he argues that architectural practice is moving from scripting, rules, and parametric systems toward search, data, machine learning, and mass customization. In Beyond Digital: Design and Automation at the End of Modernity, he extends this argument by showing how automation and artificial intelligence unsettle one of modernity’s central assumptions: that design should depend on repeatable rules, universal standards, and scalable formulas. Human intelligence often seeks rules, laws, and simplified explanations for the world; AI, by contrast, works through large datasets, correlations, and pattern recognition without necessarily understanding the underlying causes or rules. For this course, Carpo helps students ask what happens when design no longer begins with fixed rules, but with probabilistic patterns, automated variation, and new forms of judgment.
Featured Work · AI + Architecture
Architecture in the Age of Artificial Intelligence
An Introduction to AI for Architects · Bloomsbury Visual Arts, 2022
Neil Leach ↗
Leach offers the most direct survey of how AI is currently entering architectural practice — covering generative design, machine learning applications, robotics, and the implications for training, authorship, and professional identity. Unlike more theoretical accounts, this text is oriented toward practitioners and students actively working with these tools, making it a practical counterpart to the more critical readings in the course. Leach does not simply celebrate AI capability; he asks what architects need to understand about how these systems work in order to use them with intention and judgment. For the design accelerator module and the workflow audit sessions of Core Module B, this text provides the most current professional orientation — what the tools actually do and what designers need to think about when deploying them.
Featured Work · AI + Architecture
Neural Architecture
Design and Artificial Intelligence · ORO Editions, 2022
Matias del Campo ↗
Del Campo's edited collection brings together architects actively working with neural networks, GANs, and machine learning as design instruments — not as novelty, but as a serious expansion of what spatial thinking can do. The work collected here is not speculative visualization but rigorous design research: testing what these systems generate when trained on architectural typologies, structural logics, and spatial programs, and asking what the outputs reveal about both machine behavior and disciplinary assumption. For the course's short module on AI as design accelerator, del Campo's practice provides the most architecturally serious model of hybrid workflow — one that treats the machine not as a tool for speed but as a collaborator whose outputs require disciplined interpretation, selection, and development.
Invited Lecture · Core Module A
AI, Data Visualization, and Public Knowledge
Two-day lecture sequence within AI-Mediated Ethics · August 25 and 27
Jian Huang ↗
Huang's contribution brings data visualization, human-computer interaction, and public-facing systems of knowledge into the ethics module. His lectures position AI systems not as neutral tools, but as cultural and technical infrastructures that shape how information is seen, interpreted, and trusted. For students, this provides a bridge between AI literacy and design responsibility: how to read outputs critically, how to recognize bias in visualization and representation, and how to translate technical systems into accountable human-centered design judgment.
Featured Work · Short Module C · Core Module A
Duty Free Art
Art in the Age of Planetary Civil War · Verso, 2017 — with "A Sea of Data" (e-flux, 2016)
Hito Steyerl ↗
Steyerl is the sharpest contemporary thinker on the politics of images in the age of algorithmic production. Duty Free Art traces how images circulate in post-sovereign, financialized space — as assets, as weapons, as data — and what this means for art, representation, and visibility. Her essay "A Sea of Data" is more directly technical: it examines pattern recognition, apophenia, and the way machine vision produces meaning from noise, seeing connections that may or may not be there. Both texts bear on the course's ethics module and the glitch intensive: they provide a political and aesthetic theory of what AI image systems are actually doing — not capturing reality but producing a particular version of it, one shaped by the conditions of its training and the interests of its deployment.
Featured Work · Core Module A
AI as Normal Technology
An alternative to the vision of AI as a potential superintelligence
Arvind Narayanan ↗ Sayash Kapoor ↗
Narayanan and Kapoor argue that AI is best understood not as an emergent superintelligence on the verge of runaway capability, but as a class of technologies with specific strengths, real limitations, and predictable failure modes — subject to the same social, institutional, and political forces as any other tool. Rather than calibrating practice around worst-case AGI scenarios or utopian acceleration, this framing asks designers to engage AI with the same disciplined skepticism applied to any complex system: interrogating what it actually does, who benefits, what it gets wrong, and how accountability gets structured around its use. For this course, their argument grounds the ethical module: the question is not what AI might eventually become, but what it is already doing and how design practice responds to that reality.
Featured Work · Disciplinary History
Architectural Intelligence
How Designers and Architects Created the Digital Landscape · MIT Press, 2017
Molly Wright Steenson ↗
Steenson recovers the largely forgotten history of how AI entered architecture — tracing cybernetics and early computation through Nicholas Negroponte's Architecture Machine Group, Christopher Alexander's pattern languages, and Richard Saul Wurman's information architecture. Her argument is that architects were among the first to seriously experiment with machine intelligence, and that this history has been erased from both AI and design discourse. For a course asking what it means to practice with AI today, Steenson provides the disciplinary origin story: designers have been here before, and what they built shaped how the entire field thinks about human-computer interaction.
Featured Work · Core Module A
The Stack: On Software and Sovereignty
MIT Press, 2015 — with Revenge of the Real (2021)
Benjamin H. Bratton ↗
Bratton argues that computation should be understood as a planetary-scale infrastructure — a layered system of Earth, Cloud, City, Address, Interface, and User — that reorganizes territory, governance, and design agency simultaneously. Rather than treating AI as a tool that sits inside architecture, Bratton asks how software and sovereignty are already restructuring what architecture is and what it can claim to do. His later work in Revenge of the Real extends this into pandemic governance and the politics of modelling — the question of who gets to produce the image of the world that decisions are made from. Both texts bear directly on the ethics module's concern with authorship, accountability, and the political stakes of synthetic realism.
Featured Work · Core Module B
Medium Design
Knowing How to Work on the World · Verso, 2021 — with Extrastatecraft (2014)
Keller Easterling ↗
Easterling argues that the most consequential design operates not at the level of objects or buildings but at the level of systems, protocols, and active forms — the conditions that shape what other things can become. Extrastatecraft maps this through free zones, infrastructure networks, and the hidden spatial products of global logistics. Medium Design extends it into a design methodology: instead of designing outcomes, design the medium — the relational field in which outcomes become possible. For the narrative intelligence module, this reframes what a client relationship actually is: not the delivery of a solution, but the construction of conditions, shared language, and institutional capacity. Easterling gives students a framework for designing the conversation, not just the building.
Featured Work · Digital Culture
Digital Culture in Architecture
An Introduction for the Design Professions · Birkhäuser, 2010
Antoine Picon ↗
Picon provides the most rigorous architectural-historical account of what digital tools have done to design culture — not as a history of software or fabrication, but as a transformation in how architecture produces meaning, addresses bodies, and constructs ornament. Where Carpo focuses on notation and the shift from type to variation, Picon tracks aesthetic and cultural consequences: the new relationship between surface and structure, the return of complexity and pattern, and the way digital production changes how architects think about time, materiality, and representation. For a course investigating AI's design consequences, Picon supplies the critical vocabulary for asking: what does this tool change about what architecture can mean?
Featured Work · Short Module C
Art and Cosmotechnics
e-flux / University of Minnesota Press, 2021
Yuk Hui ↗
Hui argues that Western AI assumes a universal technics — a single, globally applicable model of what technology is and how it relates to human life — and that this assumption erases the plurality of cosmological relationships through which different cultures have historically understood tools, making, and intelligence. Drawing on Chinese aesthetics, philosophy of technology, and the concept of recursivity, he asks what it would mean to have multiple modernities rather than a single technological trajectory. For the glitch and uncanny module, Hui deepens the critical frame: machine error is not just an aesthetic event or a design resource — it is a site where the assumptions built into the system become briefly visible. What gets hallucinated reveals what was assumed.
Featured Work · Core Module A
Plans and Situated Actions
The Problem of Human-Machine Communication · Cambridge University Press, 1987 / 2007
Lucy Suchman ↗
Suchman's foundational study of a photocopier's help system demonstrates that AI fails not because of insufficient intelligence but because it models plans rather than situations. Machines reason from abstract representations of action; people act in response to the specific, contingent, embodied conditions in front of them. The gap between those two modes of engagement is where most AI systems break down — and where most claims about AI capability are overstated. For students designing with AI tools, Suchman provides a principled account of why these tools will always require human judgment at the point of application: not as a limitation to be overcome, but as a structural condition of how situated action works. Her argument is foundational to the ethics module's concern with authorship, disclosure, and the irreducibility of design judgment.
Practice-Based Framework · Disclosure Standards
Useful Arts / Creative AI Usage Disclosure
AI transparency, creative authorship, disclosure protocols, and professional trust
Elley Cheng ↗ Useful Arts ↗
Cheng and Useful Arts provide a practice-based framework for understanding authorship, disclosure, and trust in AI-assisted creative work. Rather than treating AI transparency as a technical footnote, this reference asks what must be made visible when design work is produced with generative tools: what is human-authored, what is AI-assisted, what sources or prompts shaped the outcome, and what clients, collaborators, instructors, and publics have the right to know. In the course, Useful Arts serves as a professional reference point for accountable AI disclosure in design education and creative practice.

AI Use Policy & Academic Integrity

Permitted Use

AI use is permitted. Invisible AI use is not.

  • Disclose which AI tools were used and how they were applied
  • Document what inputs shaped the outputs generated
  • Record how outputs were selected, modified, or rejected
  • Show where human judgment redirected the process
  • Cite written sources, visual precedent, design references, datasets, prompts, and AI-assisted content
  • Document principal sources: authors, projects, tools, dates, and platforms
Integrity Requirement

Transparency is part of the design ethic of this course.

  • Undisclosed AI-generated content presented as fully independent authorship may be treated as a breach of academic integrity
  • When in doubt — disclose the source, the tool, and the extent of assistance
  • Attendance is essential — the course depends on discussion, critique, and collaborative exchange
  • Late work may be downgraded unless prior arrangements have been made
